How the model works, and what it can't tell you

Each car is driven by standard microsimulation models: the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) for following and MOBIL for lane changes. They are standard published models with the same structure as commercial tools: Vissim uses Wiedemann car-following and Aimsun uses Gipps, so the details differ. One trait, assertiveness z, is drawn for every driver from a normal (bell) curve. It sets all of that driver's parameters together, so a pushy driver tailgates, accepts tight gaps and rarely lets anyone in. This is a simplification of the common practice of defining aggressive, normal and conservative driver types. Vissim, for example, uses separate behaviour parameter sets and distributions rather than one linked trait.

Car following (IDM)

a = a_max · [ 1 − (v/v₀)⁴ − (s*/s)² ] s* = s₀ + v·T + v·Δv / (2·√(a_max·b))

Lane changing (MOBIL) and forced merges

Change if ã_c − a_c + p·(ã_n − a_n + ã_o − a_o) > Δa_th (Δa_th = 0.4 m/s²) Safe only if ã_n ≥ −b_safe,eff b_safe,eff = b_safe · [ 1 + 0.6·max(0, 1 − d_end/L_urg) + min(1, t_wait/10) ] (capped at 6.5 m/s²)

The closing lane is a mandatory change. As a driver gets closer to the end of the lane (d_end), or waits there stopped (t_wait), they accept harder braking from the car they cut in front of. That is the forced merge described by Hidas (2005) and in the FHWA NGSIM cooperative/forced merge work. A merge that makes the new follower brake harder than 3 m/s² is counted as a forced merge.

Giving way vs. fighting for space

P(yield) = 1 / (1 + e^(1.8·(z − c))) c = 1.5 zipper panel, driver follows the signs (taking turns is the norm) c = −0.3 merger is a late "cutter": conventional panel, or a zipper driver who ignores the signs c −= 1.2 if this driver let someone in within the last 15 s

In the conventional panels, only the through-lane driver directly behind a merger (within 30 m freeway, 20 m urban) decides whether to yield, as with Vissim's cooperative braking. If a merger has been stopped for more than 60 s, the driver behind always makes room, so nobody, truck or car, waits forever. In the zipper panels the stopped-lead-car rule below does the same job.

Zipper turn-taking (zipper panels only). The compliance slider sets, for every driver in either lane, whether they follow the zipper signs. Merging alternates one from each lane. Right after a closing-lane car merges, it is the open lane's turn. Once the next through car draws alongside the lead closing-lane car, it is the closing lane's turn. The through-lane driver directly behind the lead car then decides whether to let it in, and starts holding back from about 12 m plus 1.5 s of travel. A sign follower uses c = 1.5, so at z = 0 they give way 94% of the time, at z = 1 about 71%, and at z = 2 about 29%. A driver ignoring the signs uses the cutter curve, so at z = 0 they give way about 37% of the time. A compliant closing-lane driver waits for their turn unless there is a clear gap, has waited more than 90 s, or has z above 1.3. Closing-lane drivers who find a through car alongside drop back and tuck in behind it.

A stopped lead car gets let in. When the lead car in the closing lane is stopped at the end of the lane, the through-lane driver behind it decides again every 6 s. Each time, the pressure to let it in grows: the yield curve shifts by up to 2 units of assertiveness after 20 s of waiting. Even fairly pushy drivers usually give way after a short wait, while the most aggressive can still refuse. A driver who has started making room keeps holding back until the car is in, and will brake up to 4 m/s² to let it in. This applies in both panels. Otherwise, a driver who yields brakes for the merger up to 3 m/s² (Vissim's default limit), or up to 4 m/s² in the zipper panels. A driver who refuses and has z above 0.5 closes the gap by cutting their headway to 55%. This rule is a behavioural assumption, not a calibrated one. It encodes the fairness effect agencies report: drivers let people in when everyone is taking turns, and block when they think someone is jumping the line.

Capacity calibration

Capacity here means the highest flow the single open lane can sustain, in vehicles per hour. In a work zone the useful number is the queue discharge rate: the flow through the merge once a queue has formed. It is lower than the flow at speed just before breakdown. The model's mean headway, standstill gap and acceleration were tuned so that a car-only stream at the default speeds discharges at:

The freeway target is the commonly used base capacity for a short-term 2-to-1 freeway work zone (HCM; Krammes & Lopez). The urban target is an assumption: lower speed, a short taper and a signal just upstream. Replace it with a local value where you have one. The v/c readout divides demand by the target adjusted for trucks, using the HCM heavy-vehicle factor with ET = 2.0. On the freeway the model's trucks behave close to the HCM value of ET = 2.0. The driver mix also moves real capacity: pushy drivers run shorter headways and push it up, polite ones pull it down. Closing-lane drivers in the merge zone match the through lane's speed and ease back into the next gap, so they merge while still rolling. With strict turn-taking in place, the freeway zipper discharges about 7–12% below the conventional layout, and the urban zipper about 9–12% above it. The urban result held up in every version of the model. The freeway penalty does not: it comes from courteous drivers slowing the open lane at the taper. It is sensitive to how early through drivers react, and it is larger than field studies suggest, so treat it as a modelling outcome rather than a finding.

Merging around trucks: through-lane drivers give way to a merging truck more readily (yield curve shifted by 0.8). Merging drivers won't cut in if it would make a truck brake harder than 1.5 m/s². Both match driver-behaviour findings at merges (Kondyli & Elefteriadou). Heavy vehicle defaults are 12% on the freeway and 3% urban, in line with commonly used HCM-style defaults. Ontario 400-series corridors often run well above that, so use local MTO counts where you have them. The single-unit / tractor-trailer split (35/65 freeway, 70/30 urban) is an assumption.

OTM sizes tapers on the normal posted speed. The page uses one speed slider for both the operating speed and the taper, so the layout always matches a real OTM setup. Speeds outside the OTM table (40 km/h urban, 120 km/h freeway) use the nearest table value. In the model, a closing-lane vehicle can roll into the first third of the taper while it shifts over, staying on the open side of the cone line. It must be in the through lane by then. A longer taper therefore gives a little more room to complete a rolling merge.

Scenario rules

  • Merge point. Zipper-compliant drivers stay in their lane until the last 60 m before the taper (25 m urban), then merge in turn, at the latest a third of the way into the taper. Drivers who plan to merge late pick whichever lane is moving better upstream, so the two lanes stay balanced.
  • Early vs. late mergers. In conventional panels, drivers with z > 1 (about 16% at an average mix) ride the closing lane to the end. Everyone else looks for a gap as soon as they enter. In zipper panels, the share set by the compliance slider uses both lanes too.
  • Same drivers. Each zipper/conventional pair gets the same arrival times and the same drivers. Only the signage and the behaviour it prompts differ.
  • Urban. The upstream signal releases platoons (70 s cycle, 36 s green, 1.9 s saturation headway per lane). Green time lost because the queue has backed up to the stop bar is counted as spillback.
  • Queue length runs from the merge point back to the last slow car (under 30 km/h on the freeway, under 11 km/h urban). It adds any backup past the left edge of the view, at 7 m per stopped car. In conventional panels most of that backup sits upstream, where drivers merged early into one long line.

Limits

  • Not calibrated to a site. Use it for direction and mechanism, not for design numbers.
  • Three vehicle classes with simple adjustments, one lane-change duration, no grades, no driveways or turning traffic in the urban case.
  • Results vary between runs. Press "New drivers" a few times before declaring victory.
  • Trucks still merge less smoothly than cars at the zipper merge point, because they need long gaps. Near capacity on the freeway, the zipper queues longer and loses some throughput compared with conventional, most of all with very polite, highly compliant drivers. That is a property of this model's courtesy rules, not an established result.
  • The field evidence on throughput is mixed. MnDOT found no travel-time effect, and Iowa TPF-5(438) found better use of capacity. In the urban case the model agrees: the zipper shortens the queue and gains a little throughput. On the freeway it is less favourable to the zipper than the field studies.
